Topic: Intellectual Property Law — Copyright, Patents & Trademarks
Estimated Study Duration: 1 week (target 3-hour equivalent lesson block)

SMART Goal 1 — Foundational Concepts
By the end of this module, I will be able to define intellectual property,
distinguish between copyright, patents, trademarks, and trade secrets, and
correctly identify which category applies to a given real-world example
without referencing notes.

SMART Goal 2 — Copyright
By the end of this module, I will be able to explain what copyright protects,
how it arises automatically, its duration under the Berne Convention, the
scope of fair use, and identify two landmark copyright cases and their
significance.

SMART Goal 3 — Patents
By the end of this module, I will be able to describe patentability
requirements (novelty, inventive step, industrial applicability), outline the
application process from filing to grant, and articulate the core patent
bargain — disclosure in exchange for time-limited exclusivity.

SMART Goal 4 — Trademarks
By the end of this module, I will be able to define what trademarks protect,
explain the registration process, and illustrate trademark dilution and
genericide with real-world examples.

SMART Goal 5 — Enforcement & International Framework
By the end of this module, I will be able to identify infringement elements
for each IP type, describe key enforcement mechanisms, and outline the major
international agreements (Berne, TRIPS, Paris Convention, WIPO) and their
role in setting minimum protection standards across member states.

--- SESSION SUMMARY ---

Student has strong practical motivation but zero legal training. Key gaps:
conflation of copyright and trademark, no understanding of patent procedure,
and no awareness that IP protection is territorial.

MENTOR GUIDANCE FOR CONTENT GENERATION:

1. Anchor Concept: Open with the "bundle of rights" mental model before any
   specific IP type. Use a concrete example — buying a book does not transfer
   its copyright — to separate physical ownership from IP ownership.

2. Copyright: Emphasize automatic protection as the key surprise for most
   students. Use a flowchart covering creation → protection → expiry. Give
   fair use its own slide with the four-factor test and one real case
   (Campbell v. Acuff-Rose).

3. Patents: Build a step-by-step procedural flowchart: invention → prior art
   search → filing → examination → grant → expiry. Lead with the patent
   bargain concept — it makes the procedure meaningful. Avoid claim-drafting
   technicality.

4. Trademarks: Open with instantly recognizable brand examples. Contrast
   trademarks from copyright clearly — trademarks protect commercial origin
   signals, not creative expression. Include genericide examples (Escalator,
   Thermos) as a memorable teaching anchor.

5. Comparison Table (Critical): A dedicated summary slide comparing all three
   IP types across: what is protected, how it arises, duration, registration
   required, and renewal. This is likely the single most useful takeaway.

6. Enforcement: Use a decision-tree flowchart: detect infringement → cease
   and desist → negotiation → litigation → remedies. Include DMCA takedowns
   as a contemporary, relevant example.

7. International Framework: Frame as "why your protection stops at the
   border" then introduce Berne, Paris Convention, TRIPS, and WIPO. Close
   with the pharmaceutical patents vs. generic medicines tension as a
   compelling real-world stakes example.

8. Visual Priority: Flowcharts for copyright lifecycle and patent process;
   decision tree for enforcement; comparison table for all three IP types;
   world map of treaty membership; landmark case timeline.

KEY LEARNING OUTCOME (for course outline generation):
   By the end of this module, the student should be able to identify which
   IP protection applies to a given work, invention, or brand asset; explain
   key requirements and duration for each type; describe main enforcement
   options; and assess basic IP risk in a startup or creative project scenario.
