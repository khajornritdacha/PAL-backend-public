Goal

Topic: Climate Change — Causes, Feedback Loops & Global Policy Responses
Estimated Study Duration: 1 week (target 3-hour equivalent lesson block)

SMART Goal 1 — Causes
By the end of this module, I will be able to identify and explain the primary
natural and anthropogenic drivers of climate change, including the greenhouse
effect, CO2 and methane emissions, deforestation, and ocean heat absorption,
well enough to explain each cause to a peer without referencing notes.

SMART Goal 2 — Feedback Loops
By the end of this module, I will be able to describe at least four major
climate feedback mechanisms (ice-albedo, water vapor, permafrost methane,
cloud feedbacks), correctly classifying each as positive or negative feedback,
and draw a labeled feedback loop diagram for at least two of them from memory.

SMART Goal 3 — Tipping Points & Systems Thinking
By the end of this module, I will be able to define climate tipping points,
name at least three examples (e.g., Amazon dieback, West Antarctic Ice Sheet,
Atlantic circulation collapse), and explain how crossing a tipping point can
trigger cascading effects across interconnected Earth systems.

SMART Goal 4 — Global Policy Responses
By the end of this module, I will be able to compare and contrast the major
international climate policy frameworks — the Kyoto Protocol, the Paris
Agreement, and the COP process — evaluating their mechanisms, commitments,
and limitations, and articulate at least two market-based instruments (carbon
tax, cap-and-trade) in plain language.

SMART Goal 5 — Mitigation vs. Adaptation
By the end of this module, I will be able to distinguish mitigation strategies
(reducing emissions: renewables, electrification, carbon capture) from
adaptation strategies (responding to impact: flood defenses, drought-resistant
crops, managed retreat) and apply this distinction to evaluate a real-world
national policy example.

Prior Knowledge: General high school science background; familiar with basic
chemistry (greenhouse gases) but not with feedback systems or policy
frameworks at any depth.

Motivation: I want to engage confidently in climate policy debates, understand
news reporting on climate negotiations (e.g., COP summits), and apply
systems-thinking skills to other complex global problems.

Discussion with mentor

The student came in with strong motivation around real-world relevance —
specifically wanting to understand climate news coverage and policy debates —
but with limited background beyond high school science. The main gaps
identified were: (1) no prior exposure to systems thinking or feedback loop
diagrams, (2) confusion between weather and climate at the mechanistic level,
and (3) no awareness of how international policy instruments actually work
(e.g., the difference between a carbon tax and cap-and-trade).

MENTOR GUIDANCE FOR CONTENT GENERATION:

1. Sequencing: Start with a clear conceptual anchor — the difference between
   weather and climate — before introducing the greenhouse effect. Many
   students conflate the two, which undermines everything downstream.
   Establish this distinction on the very first slide or section.

2. Feedback Loops: This is the conceptually hardest section. The student
   finds abstract systems notation confusing. Favor concrete, real-world
   examples first (e.g., melting Arctic ice exposing darker ocean surface),
   then introduce the diagram. Use the ice-albedo feedback as the teaching
   example because it is the most visually intuitive. Avoid introducing more
   than two feedback loops per slide.

3. Tipping Points: Frame tipping points using a physical analogy first —
   e.g., a ball balanced on a hill — before applying it to climate. This
   scaffolds the abstraction. Include a world map showing approximate
   locations of major tipping elements; the spatial dimension significantly
   aids retention here.

4. Policy Section: The student has no economics background. Avoid jargon-
   heavy finance language. Explain carbon pricing with a simple supply-
   demand intuition before comparing Kyoto vs. Paris. A comparison table
   (Kyoto vs. Paris: scope, enforcement, temperature targets, participation)
   is strongly recommended. Highlight the NDC (Nationally Determined
   Contributions) mechanism as the key structural innovation of Paris.

5. Mitigation vs. Adaptation: Treat this as the synthesis section. The
   student specifically asked to understand "what countries are actually
   doing." Use real country examples — Germany (Energiewende), Bangladesh
   (adaptation planning), Netherlands (coastal management) — to make policy
   concrete. Avoid generic lists; anchor every strategy to a real case.

6. Tone and Register: The student engages well with narrative framing.
   Where possible, open each section with a question or a striking data
   point (e.g., "2023 was the hottest year in 125,000 years of recorded
   proxy data") rather than a definition. This keeps the lesson from feeling
   like a textbook.

7. Visual Priority: This topic is inherently visual. Every section should
   include at least one diagram or data visualization. Recommended visuals:
   - Keeling Curve (atmospheric CO2 since 1958)
   - Diagram of the greenhouse effect with labeled energy flows
   - Feedback loop diagrams (ice-albedo, water vapor)
   - Map of climate tipping elements
   - Temperature anomaly graph (IPCC AR6 baseline)
   - Comparison infographic: mitigation vs. adaptation strategies

KEY LEARNING OUTCOME (for course outline generation):
   By the end of this module, the student should be able to explain why the
   climate system responds non-linearly to emissions, describe the main
   feedback mechanisms that amplify warming, and critically evaluate the
   architecture and limitations of the Paris Agreement — well enough to
   participate in an informed discussion of climate policy.
